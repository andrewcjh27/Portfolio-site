01 Personal product · Web app

Momuk

오늘 뭐 먹지 — a menu suggester that answers what to eat, not where. It reads weather, region and time of day, scores 142 dishes on effects measured from ten years of Naver searches, and shows the math behind its pick.

Role

Timeline

Team
Personal project
Tools

Product DesignWeb AppRecommendation
At a glance

One dish for this moment — and the numbers behind it

Product
오늘 뭐 먹지 — a Korean menu suggester that answers what to eat, not where
Question
Can the food folklore everyone repeats — 전 on rainy days, 삼계탕 on 복날 — be measured and used?
My part
Solo: Figma design, front end, data pipeline, analysis
Built
A static web app on GitHub Pages, scored by a PySpark pipeline on Databricks that refreshes every week, for $0
Result
142 dishes ranked on effects measured from ten years of Naver searches and weather; every pick can show its own math
The problem

Map apps answer where to eat. Nothing answers what — the 오늘 뭐 먹지 that comes up every day. Momuk gives one confident suggestion for the moment you are in: the weather, the hour, who you’re with and what you want to spend.

Phase 01 · Aug 2026

Design the flow, then a rules engine — on purpose

Designed in Figma: three questions (how many, how hungry, budget) and one answer. A single bowl travels through the flow and fills as you answer — a progress bar that never looks like one — and steams at the pick.

With no users there was no data to learn from, and a model trained on nothing is a worse rules engine that can’t explain itself. So v1 was a transparent weighted sum: it samples instead of always taking the top dish, so the same weather doesn’t always give the same answer, and every culture-based rule (rain, fine dust, payday, season) was flagged folklore — to be tested later.
Phase 02 · Oct 2026

Don’t wait for users — test the folklore now

The plan was to test the folklore after ~200 logged decisions, months away. Public data could answer a population-level version immediately, and turned the project into a real data build. Constraints: $0, data that represents Korean eaters, and three skills to practise — forecasting, causal inference, experiment design.

Delivery-order data was out (not reachable from the US, now sold), so Naver search trends became the signal: free, national, daily since 2016, and a measure of craving — right for a “what do I feel like” app. Google Trends added provinces; Open-Meteo added ten years of weather for all 17.
Phase 03 · Oct – Nov 2026

Most of the work was making the data mean what it says

Every request is scaled to its own peak. Naver returns 0–100 per request, so dishes from different requests can’t be compared. Putting one mid-popularity anchor dish (김치찌개) in every request put all dishes on one scale.

Nobody searches “후라이드치킨”. Near-zero dishes were a keyword problem, not a popularity one. A keyword check kept a search term only if it’s spelled like the dish and moves like it week to week. 치킨 alone is searched ~36× more, so three chicken dishes became one.

Catching my own mistakes. 곰탕’s regional lean (×3.68 in Jeollanam-do) was circular — its search term had become 나주곰탕 — so it was withdrawn. And a test of whether regional leans were just tourists found no seasonal visitor pattern, so they stayed.
Findings · weather & region

The folklore mostly holds — and the data knows Korea’s map

Against dry days in the same month and weekday, the five most rain-sensitive dishes were exactly the ones hand-tagged 비오는날: 김치전 ×1.79, 해물파전 ×1.74, 수제비 ×1.34. The data also found rainy-day soups the tags missed (짬뽕, 마라탕).

Without being told a single regional fact, province searches recovered local specialties: 막국수 in Gangwon ×5.18, 밀면 in Busan ×4.01, 콩나물국밥 in Jeollabuk-do ×2.78. Weekends brought hangover food (콩나물국밥 ×1.59) and day-trip food (매운탕 ×1.67).
Findings · food days

복날 is a chicken day, and Christmas is a date night

Each food day was compared with the same weekday 1–3 weeks around it, across ten years. 복날 → 삼계탕 ×9.73, 설날 → 떡국 ×7.76, and 동지 → 팥죽 a startling ×66.

The surprises were better than the folklore: on 복날 every chicken dish rises (백숙 ×4.95, 닭한마리 ×3.02); Christmas tops out at steak and pasta, above chicken and pizza. One rule failed — 추석 → 김치전 came out ×0.75, because holiday 전 are 동그랑땡 and 모둠전, not the everyday 김치전.

The findings report true sizes; the recommender caps any one day at ×3 so it can’t take over the list.
Phase 04 · Nov 2026

Same app, guesses replaced by measurements

v2 keeps the design and the three questions, and swaps every guessed rule for a measured one: popularity, rain, temperature, region, meal time, weekend and food days. Scoring in log space keeps each factor a separate multiplier, so 왜 이 음식이야? shows the real calculation (비 오는 날 ×1.4, 지역 · 서울 ×1.3 …), not copy.

Testing it like a user changed the rules: late night and breakfast became filters (밀면 at 1 a.m. can’t be had), delivery food counts for late-night solo eaters, a turned-down dish never comes back, and after five “안 땡겨” it asks a narrowing question. A weekly Databricks job writes one JSON file to GitHub, so the site stays static and free.
Outcome & reflection

Live at andrewcjh27.github.io/Momuk in Korean and English, with anonymous, cookie-free counting of which picks people accept. Every choice — and every alternative rejected — is in a public decision log.

Next: log each suggestion and whether it was accepted, then A/B test population picks against personalised ones by acceptance rate — and forecast tomorrow’s cravings from the weather forecast.

02 Interactive installation · Spatial UX

Urban Conga

Designed a data-informed public installation system that measures how people actually use civic, park, and commercial spaces — from behavioral survey to Rhino layout.

Role
UX Design Consultant
Timeline
Jan – Jun 2026
Team
9 consultants · Invention Corps of Berkeley
Tools
Rhino 3D, FigJam, survey analysis
UX ResearchData SchemasRhino 3DSpatial Design
The problem

Urban Conga’s installations had no way to show how people engaged with them. The goal: capture real usage patterns with low-cost sensing, without making people uncomfortable in public.

Phase 01 · Jan – Feb 2026

Public-space behavioral survey

Built and distributed a survey across the team’s networks asking people how they actually use civic squares, parks, and commercial spaces — group size, time of day, length of stay, deal-breakers, and what they’d feel comfortable doing in public. The goal: capture real behavior, not assumptions, before any installation design started.
Phase 02 · Feb 2026

Insight extraction

Each of the 9 consultants was asked to surface 2–3 patterns from the survey responses. The themes that emerged immediately:
  • Almost everyone visits public spaces in groups of 3–5 people
  • Overcrowding, cleanliness, and noise are the top deal-breakers
  • People are uncomfortable with anything they’re unaware of — but much more relaxed once they understand what an installation is doing
  • Behavior splits into two distinct modes: quick passers-by (15–30 min) and extended visitors (1+ hour)
Phase 03 · Feb – Mar 2026

Space-type segmentation

The team recognized that one installation concept can’t cover every context. We grouped responses into three space archetypes — each with its own usage signature, mood, and core values:
  • Civic spaces — 2 hours, 2–5 people, productive at noon/night, valuing education, focus, and sightseeing
  • Park spaces — 30 min–1 hour, relaxation-focused, valuing exercise, stress relief, quiet, and friends
  • Commercial spaces — 1–2 hours, productive at noon/night, social and purchase-driven
Phase 04 · Mar 2026

Concept ideation

Each space archetype got its own installation direction. The brainstorm produced a long-list of physical and digital interaction concepts matched to the space’s mood:
  • Litter walls, locks, etching/scratching — permanence and trace-leaving in civic spaces
  • Infrared sensing & AI cluster detection — measuring how long and how many, depending on context
  • Touch / pressure / button voting — e.g. a Starbucks “Strawberry Acái vs. Caramel Macchiato first to 100” in a mall
  • Material wear-and-tear over time — the installation visibly changes from use
  • Sticker walls / community blurbs, kinetic park installations, racing/climbing micro-games
Phase 05 · Apr 2026

Sensing & data-schema constraints

Translated the qualitative behavioral goals (“measure dwell time,” “count cluster size,” “detect voluntary engagement”) into structured, quantifiable data schemas with low-cost, low-overhead sensing approaches — capacitive touch, IR proximity, simple pressure switches.
Phase 06 · May – Jun 2026

Spatial & 3D layout in Rhino

Modeled candidate installation footprints in Rhino 3D, validating that each layout could host the inferred data flows from phase 5 while staying within the budget and footprint of a real public space. Closed the loop between behavior → sensing → physical form.

Todo Widget

A to-do list that lives in the Mac menu bar — daily, weekly and monthly lists, a planner for each day of the week, and D-day countdowns for deadlines. Released free for anyone to install.

A to-do list that lives one click away

Product
A macOS menu bar app: daily, weekly and monthly lists, a planner for each weekday, and D-day countdowns
Goal
Let anyone install it free — with no paid Apple developer account — and be able to show it’s real
My part
Solo: design, SwiftUI build, packaging and release
Built
v1.0.0 and v1.0.1 on GitHub Releases, a one-line installer, automated builds and an app icon
Stack
Swift 6, SwiftUI, Swift Package Manager, GitHub Actions
The problem

A to-do list only works if you look at it. Todo Widget puts it in the Mac menu bar — always one click away, never another window to open — and splits tasks by horizon: today, this week, this month.

Build · Mar 2026

Three horizons in one small window

Built as a SwiftUI menu bar app with a Swift package, step by step: the menu bar entry point, a data model saved on the Mac, then the list — add, check off, rename, delete.

Weekly and monthly tasks can take a due date that becomes a D-day countdown, colour-coded: blue ahead, orange on the day, red when overdue. Daily tasks stay deliberately simple.
Planner · Apr 2026

A plan for each day of the week

A switch in the corner turns the widget into a weekly planner with a tab per day — classes on Monday, errands on Saturday — without a second app or window.
Release · Nov 2026

Shipping it free, without Apple’s $99 program

Warning-free installs need notarization, which needs the paid Apple Developer Program, and Homebrew now disables apps that fail those checks. So the release uses what’s free: a free (ad-hoc) signature, GitHub Releases with public download counts, and a one-line Terminal installer that puts the app in Applications and opens it.

Tagging a version builds and publishes a release automatically on GitHub Actions. It hides from the Dock, offers Launch at login as an opt-in checkbox, and ships under the MIT license.
What broke along the way

Shipping is where the bugs were

A compiler crash. The first release build crashed Swift 6.3 itself on one line of the launch-at-login toggle; passing a closure instead of a method reference fixed it.

Tasks would have vanished. The packaged app saves under a new identity, so existing lists would look empty after installing — a one-time migration copies them across on first launch.

Two icons in the menu bar. An old development build kept relaunching itself at login; found with ps and launchctl, stopped, and its launch file set aside rather than deleted.

A page that didn’t earn its place. A separate download page was built, then dropped for a README with screenshots and install, use and uninstall steps.
Outcome & reflection

Public and free since November 2026: v1.0.0, then v1.0.1 with its own app icon. Anyone on macOS 13 or later can install it with one command, and every release shows how many times it’s been downloaded.

Next: an opt-in, anonymous “app opened” ping, so success is measured in weekly users rather than downloads.

04 Browser extension · Research to UI

Mozilla Firefox

Researched how people feel about trading performance for battery life, then designed and built a Firefox extension that makes tab memory and CPU use visible.

Role
IT Consultant (research & UI)
Timeline
Aug 2025 – Feb 2026
Team
12 consultants · Invention Corps of Berkeley
Tools
Figma, FigJam, React, Vite
User ResearchSurvey DesignFigmaReactVite
The problem

Firefox wanted to explore an Adaptive Performance mode that throttles idle tabs. The open questions: do users care about eco features, what would convince them, and how much speed would they give up for battery?

Phase 01 · Aug 2025

Project framing — Adaptive Performance

Started from a hypothesis: most browsers waste battery on idle tabs, and Firefox could differentiate with an Adaptive Performance mode that intelligently throttles based on usage. Defined the open questions we’d need to answer with users: do they care about eco features, what data would convince them, and how much performance loss is acceptable in exchange for battery gain?
Phase 02 · Sep 2025 (Week 1)

Survey question brainstorm

The 12-person team filled a FigJam board with raw interview/survey question candidates — each consultant contributing from their angle. Questions ranged from blunt (“why do you prefer your browser over Firefox?”) to specific (“if you could save 10% battery at the cost of significantly laggier performance, would you?”).
Phase 03 · Sep 2025

Categorize into themes

Grouped the brainstorm into five working buckets that became the spine of the eventual questionnaire:
  • Background / Demographics — age, browser of choice, hardware, power-user level
  • Eco Qs — environmental motivation, willingness to trade speed for sustainability
  • Power Qs — current habits around tab sleeping, low-power modes, memory awareness
  • Convenience Qs — 1-click vs. customizable, visibility preferences for feature output
  • Extensions — how integrated browser extensions are into the user’s daily flow
Phase 04 · Oct 2025

Survey structuring

Pressure-tested the brainstorm against eight guiding criteria — purpose, target audience, question types, clarity, flow/logic, scalability, time-to-complete, and ethics. The result: a coherent survey that flows from general to specific, mixes qualitative and quantitative items, and stays under a respondent-friendly length.
Phase 05 · Oct – Nov 2025

Final survey assembly & distribution

Consolidated the brainstorm into a clean, multi-section instrument: Background Screening, Eco-Conscious Preferences, Power-user Questions, Extensions, Browser Usage & Experience, and Final Questions. Distributed across the team’s networks; collected enough responses to read both broad signals and edge cases.
Phase 06 · Dec 2025 – Feb 2026

Build — tab-performance extension

Took the survey insights into engineering. Built a Firefox extension in React + Vite that tracks 50+ concurrent tabs in real time and lets users filter and sort by memory and CPU usage — the “visibility” signal the survey identified as the missing piece in current eco-mode UIs. Designed the UI in Figma with the 12-person team split across frontend and backend tracks.

The insight

Braven assumed students needed more career information. They didn’t. Students picked virtual events because they were short on time — and what they actually wanted was direct, 1:1 contact with mentors and employers, with needs that changed sharply by class year.

Research · Jan – Mar 2025

One survey, four very different students

We surveyed undergraduates at public four-year universities, charted the responses, and affinity-mapped them in FigJam. One general persona proved too coarse to design for, so we split the data by class year. Three findings shaped everything after:
  • Time is the barrier. Convenience was overwhelmingly why students chose virtual events at all.
  • They want the outcome, not the homework. Students wanted the end result but lacked the steps to get there.
  • Needs shift by year. First- and second-years need direction and low-pressure contact; third- and fourth-years want resume help and direct access to employers.

Lead with a roadmap, not a content feed

Because students “want the result but lack the steps,” the app opens on a personalised Career Roadmap and My Tasks — a short list of next actions — instead of a feed of events and videos. A short Career Questionnaire sets the roadmap, so one app serves all four class years.
